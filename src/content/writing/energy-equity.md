I gave a version of this talk at Africa Business Forum in Tallinn in 2023. The framework has been refined since, but the core idea hasn't changed.

The energy gap in Africa is not primarily a technology problem. Solar panels and wind turbines are mature technologies. The problem is capital — patient, structured, long-term capital that accepts African country risk.

## The four-part framework

**Equity participation.** Government takes a stake in the project. Not a royalty, not a tax incentive — a real equity position that pays dividends as the project generates revenue. This means the government's interest is aligned with the project's success from day one.

**Free power to citizens.** A portion of the project's output is allocated to the local grid at cost. Not free to the utility — free to end users. Citizens get reliable power without the government having to budget for it.

**Local jobs.** Construction, operations, maintenance. Not as a PR exercise — as a genuine economic development programme with training pathways.

**Food security.** Where relevant, co-locate the energy project with agricultural infrastructure. Solar-powered irrigation, cold storage, processing facilities. Energy and food security addressed together.

The cost to government: zero upfront. The commitment from the developer: real equity dilution.

## Why this works

The standard model — sovereign debt, EPC contracts, utility tariffs — has failed at scale in Africa for 40 years. Not because the projects weren't viable. Because the financial structure creates misaligned incentives.

When a government has no equity stake, it has no incentive to resolve the bottlenecks that kill projects: grid interconnection delays, land disputes, import duty disputes, currency risk.

When a government has an equity stake, those bottlenecks become its problem to solve.

## Patient capital is the only kind that works here

The objection I hear most: this structure requires developers who can absorb long payback periods. Yes. That's the point.

> Sustainable energy should be a fundamental right — accessible to all, financed by the markets it serves.

The capital that can absorb long paybacks is either development finance institution capital or patient private capital. Both exist. Neither is currently deployed at the right structure in Africa.

The market gap is not a technology gap. It's a structuring gap.
